## 1. Read the board before drafting anything

Treat every negotiation as a position, not a conversation. Before writing
a reply, answer these silently:

- **Their real BATNA vs. stated BATNA.** What they say they'll do if you
  don't move ("we'll build it ourselves", "I have another offer") vs.
  what they can actually do. Look for tells: mentions of an overloaded
  colleague, a vague competing option with no name/date, hedging language.
  A named, dated, specific alternative is a real BATNA. A vague one is a
  bluff or a hope.
- **Your BATNA.** What happens if this deal doesn't close. Be honest about
  how much you need it -- that changes how hard you can hold.
- **Tempo.** Who has a deadline? Whoever needs to close faster has less
  leverage. Note it, don't announce it.
- **What they're optimizing for, if not price.** Budget ceiling, social
  proof to their own boss/client, speed, risk reduction, relationship.
  Price is rarely the only variable in play even when it's the only one
  they name.
- **The concession history.** Count how many times you've already moved
  in this conversation. Two concessions in a row, or a third ask for
  "your lowest," is a request to keep bidding against yourself. Recognize
  it and stop.

## 2. The core rule: concede scope, not price

Never discount the same deliverable twice. If the number needs to move,
the product changes size with it -- fewer pages, fewer revisions, shorter
warranty, phased delivery. This does three things: protects the rate card
for future clients, gives the other side a real reason the number is
different, and stops this negotiation from becoming the new anchor for
what you charge.

When someone asks "what's your final/lowest price," do not answer with a
new number reflexively. Options, in order of preference:

1. **Hold and point to the existing smaller option.** "The price on the
   full scope is what I sent. The way the number moves is the job gets
   smaller, which is why I already sent [smaller package]." No new
   number, no new concession, ball back in their court.
2. **Trade, don't cut.** If you do move price, tie it to something of
   equal value: a retainer, a referral commitment, a faster decision
   date, a testimonial, first-mover access to a portfolio credit.
3. **One firm move, then hold.** If you must give a number, give it once,
   state the reason (hours, scope, delivery time), and stop. Don't
   pre-concede your next move by hinting a lower number exists.

## 3. Tone: warm door, firm floor

The message that holds price should not read as cold or defensive. Two
moves do most of the work:

- **Concede the small, cheap thing generously.** If they defended a
  colleague's past work, their taste, or their instinct -- agree with it
  plainly and specifically. This costs nothing and lowers their guard
  without moving on the thing that matters (price/scope).
- **Leave the door open without chasing.** Closing lines should invite
  them back ("whenever you're ready," "happy to talk it through") without
  a deadline pressure tactic layered on top unless urgency is real and
  disclosed honestly. A warm, patient close reads as confidence. A
  countdown-clock close reads as a tactic and erodes trust with repeat
  local clients faster than it closes deals.

## 4. Persuasion tactics worth using (and their limits)

Drawn from established negotiation practice (Chris Voss / FBI hostage
negotiation tactical empathy, Harvard Program on Negotiation BATNA/ZOPA
framing, and the Jimmy McGill/Saul Goodman archetype as a case study in
persuasive reframing -- not reproduced dialogue, just the underlying
technique):

| Tactic | What it is | When it's honest to use | Where it backfires |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labeling / tactical empathy | Naming the other side's position out loud ("it sounds like budget is the real constraint here") before responding to it | Almost always -- it de-escalates and surfaces the real issue | Never really backfires if genuine; reads as manipulative if used as a stalling trick |
| Calibrated questions | "How am I supposed to do that?" / "What makes this the right price for you?" -- shifts the burden of solving the gap onto them | When you want them to reveal their real ceiling or justify their number | Overused, sounds evasive |
| Reframe the problem, not just the price | Turn "your price is too high" into "what does this need to include for the price to make sense" | Always -- this is the scope-not-price rule from section 2 | N/A, this is the core move |
| Strategic self-deprecation / humor to disarm | Light, specific, non-generic warmth before the firm part of the message | Good for relationship-based repeat clients (small local markets) | Careful in first-contact or high-stakes formal negotiations (comp with a new employer) -- reads as unserious |
| Manufactured urgency | Fake scarcity or fake deadlines | **Don't.** In a small market (KRI, repeat clients) a bluffed deadline that's later exposed costs the relationship permanently | Always, unless the urgency is real (a quote validity date is fine because it's true) |
| The "one more thing" / anchoring reveal | Letting the other side think the conversation is closing before landing the actual ask | Fine as a rhetorical device to reduce defensiveness, not to hide unfavorable terms | Don't use to bury bad news (hidden fees, scope cuts they'd object to if surfaced directly) |
| Reading what they're not saying | Behzad-style tells: a vague in-house alternative, a colleague named as "overloaded," hedged language about timelines | Always -- this is BATNA-reading, not manipulation | N/A |

**The honesty line:** tactics that create false information (fake
deadlines, invented competing offers, hidden terms) are off the table
regardless of effectiveness -- this system operates in relationship-repeat
local markets where a caught bluff ends future work permanently. Tactics
that surface real information faster (labeling, calibrated questions,
scope reframing) are always fair game.

## 5. Salary/comp negotiation specifics (job offers)

For `oferta`/`ofertas` context, apply the same board-reading before
drafting a counter:

- Anchor high but inside a credible band -- cite market data or a
  competing signal if one exists, don't invent one.
- Never give a number first if the recruiter asks for your range and you
  can defer it ("What's the budgeted range for this role?").
- If pressed for a floor, give a range, not a single number, and tie the
  top of the range to scope of responsibility, not just base pay.
- Trade non-cash terms (start date, signing bonus, review timeline,
  remote days, title) when cash is capped -- same "concede scope not
  price" logic applies to comp packages.

## 6. Output format

When drafting a negotiation reply:

1. State the board read first (their BATNA, tempo, what they're really
   optimizing for, concession count so far) in 2-4 lines, before drafting.
2. Recommend one move, not a menu of three -- this is chess, not a
   catalog.
3. Draft the actual message the user will send, in the language/register
   they use with that contact, short unless asked otherwise.
4. Note the internal negotiation state (floor, what's been conceded, what
   trade would justify the next move) separately from the client-facing
   text, so a future session can pick the thread back up.

## 7. Script shelf (grounded in `library/custom/books/never-split-the-difference.md` + `lets-get-real.md`)

Reach for these before improvising a reply:

- **The universal deflection-counter:** "How am I supposed to do that?" —
  a polite "no" that hands them your constraint to solve. Use BEFORE naming
  any counter-number against a lowball.
- **Accusation audit opener** (pre-empt the objection): "You might be
  thinking this is high for the local market…" — name it first, deflate it.
- **Label + mirror** instead of arguing: "Sounds like the budget got
  squeezed" / repeat their last 3 key words as a question. They elaborate;
  you learn the real interest.
- **Ackerman ladder** when haggling is unavoidable: plan 65% → 85% → 95% →
  100% of your target, decreasing steps, non-round final number, small
  non-cash sweetener at the floor. Never deviate mid-exchange.
- **Three-part money response** (when asked "what does this cost?" too
  early): "I don't know your exact number yet — similar engagements land
  between X and Y — is that the neighborhood you're planning in?"
- **The dead-thread reviver** (one sentence, no pleasantries): "Have you
  given up on {fixing the project / this role / the rebrand}?" — highest
  reply rate of any follow-up format.
- **Silence discipline:** after you state a number, stop typing. The next
  concession belongs to whoever speaks first — never you (self-bid rule §above).
- **Register check before sending:** KRI counterpart → warmth around a firm
  number, concede ceremony not fee; Western counterpart → shorter, direct,
  criteria-based (`library/communication.md`).
