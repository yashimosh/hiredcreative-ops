# Positioning — What You Sell, To Whom, As What

**Sources:** April Dunford — *Obviously Awesome*; Blair Enns — *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto*; David C. Baker — *The Business of Expertise*.
**Your current position:** define this once in `modes/_profile.md` and don't re-derive it every session — everything downstream (evaluations, outreach, proposals) should read from that single statement.
**Deep sources (full distillations):** `library/custom/books/obviously-awesome.md`, `win-without-pitching.md`, `business-of-expertise.md`, `1-page-marketing-plan.md`.

## Dunford — positioning is context-setting

- Positioning = the context that makes your value obvious. Weak positioning forces the buyer to figure out what you are; they won't.
- Five components, set in order: **competitive alternatives** (what they'd do without you — often "do nothing" or "cousin who designs") → **unique attributes** (what you have that alternatives don't) → **value** (what those attributes enable, in their terms) → **who cares most** (best-fit segment) → **market category** (the frame that makes it all click).
- Pick the category that makes your strengths obvious, not the biggest one. "AI-native creative director" ≠ "designer" ≠ "AI agency" — each frame changes what you're compared against and what price is normal.
- Test: if a prospect hears the one-liner and asks the *right next question* (price, availability, process — not "so what do you do?"), the position works.

## Baker — expertise economics

- Expertise = pattern recognition from seeing the same problem many times. Positioning narrow enough to see repeat patterns is what creates real (chargeable) expertise.
- The narrower the focus, the fewer competitors, the less the buyer can compare on price, and the more you can diagnose from across the table.
- Horizontal (a craft: brand identity) × vertical (a market: KRI businesses / MENA NGOs) — you need at least one axis narrow. Both narrow = strongest.
- Fewer, larger clients. The goal is opportunity flow you can decline — selectivity signals expertise.

## Enns — the practitioner's posture (12 proclamations, compressed)

1. Specialize deep enough that prospects can't easily substitute you.
2. Replace presentations with conversations — diagnose, don't perform.
3. Diagnose before prescribing; prescribing without diagnosis is malpractice.
4. Don't do free strategy work in pursuit of the sale; free *diagnosis snippet* as proof-of-looking is fine, free *solutions* are not. The 1-page audit shows observations, never the fix.
5. Address money early — price talk in the first real conversation, not the proposal reveal.
6. Refuse to bid against a spec sheet; if they've already decided the solution, you're a vendor.
7. Be willing to walk; selectivity is the only credible signal of expertise.
8. Proposals confirm what was already agreed in conversation — never explore new ground.

## Point of view — the sharp edge

- An expert has a POV: an opinion about how this problem *should* be solved that some prospects will disagree with. A position nobody could disagree with is not a position.
- Example POV: "agency-grade creative at studio-of-one speed, because AI is wielded by a creative director, not sold by a technician." Demo-first — proof is the work itself, never a deck. Write your own equivalent in `modes/_profile.md`.
- Every deliverable (outreach line, proposal page, portfolio caption) should be recognizably from *this* practitioner. If it could be from any freelancer, rewrite.

## Anti-patterns (auto-flag in any draft)

- Listing services as a menu ("branding, logos, websites, AI, social media…") — menu = generalist = price shopping.
- Leading with tools ("I use Midjourney/Claude…") — tools are alternatives-thinking bait.
- Positioning by adjective ("premium", "creative", "innovative") instead of by segment + problem + alternative.
- Chasing a market where the position doesn't transfer — some buyers/employers structurally cannot value expertise the way you're positioned; recognize and skip them rather than reshaping the position to fit.
