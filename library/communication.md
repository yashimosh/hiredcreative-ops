# Communication — Register, Listening, Cross-Cultural Delivery

**Sources:** Dale Carnegie — *How to Win Friends and Influence People*; Erin Meyer — *The Culture Map*; Maister, Green & Galford — *The Trusted Advisor*.
**Deep sources (full distillations):** `library/custom/books/how-to-win-friends.md`, `culture-map.md`, `trusted-advisor.md`.
**Relationship to other files:** this is the delivery layer — HOW to say what `outreach.md`, `sales-conversations.md`, and `negotiation.md` tell you to say. Read this when the same message needs to land differently across a KRI client, a Western NGO officer, and a remote hiring manager in the same week.

## Core thesis

- The content of a message and the register it's delivered in are separable, and register is where most cross-cultural failures happen — not substance.
- Trust and clarity both compound faster through listening than through talking. The trust equation (`trusted-advisor.md`) puts self-orientation in the denominator — every word spent on yourself instead of them divides the total.
- Nothing here is culture-neutral. Every register choice (directness, formality, pacing, feedback style) is calibrated to a specific counterpart, not applied uniformly.

## The listening stack (Carnegie + Maister, fused)

1. **Become genuinely interested first.** Not a technique — the posture underneath every other move. People spend more energy thinking about themselves than anything else; interest in them is scarce and valuable.
2. **Let them do most of the talking** (aim ≥60/40 in any discovery or trust-building conversation). People trust conclusions they arrive at themselves more than ones handed to them.
3. **Reflect before responding.** Summarize their point accurately enough that they say "that's right" (not just "you're right") — that's the signal you've actually understood, not just waited for your turn.
4. **Name the hard thing gently, once.** Maister's "naming and claiming": acknowledge the awkwardness of raising an issue, take responsibility for raising it, then state it plainly with no further hedging. Works for scope creep, budget gaps, missed deadlines, resume gaps.

## The 8 scales (Meyer) — position is relative, not absolute

Every scale below matters only in RELATION to whoever you're talking to — a "direct" culture to one counterpart reads as "indirect" to another. Recalibrate per relationship, don't apply a fixed map.

| Scale | Poles | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Communicating | Low-context ↔ High-context | Do words carry full meaning, or does meaning live in shared context/subtext? |
| Evaluating | Direct negative feedback ↔ Indirect | Is criticism stated plainly or wrapped in softeners/praise-sandwiches? |
| Persuading | Principles-first ↔ Applications-first | Do you build the theory before the conclusion, or lead with the practical takeaway? |
| Leading | Egalitarian ↔ Hierarchical | Is the boss a first-among-equals or a clear authority to defer to? |
| Deciding | Consensual ↔ Top-down | Does a decision need group buy-in first, or does the leader decide and the group executes? |
| Trusting | Task-based (cognitive) ↔ Relationship-based (affective) | Is trust earned through delivered work, or through personal rapport built over time? |
| Disagreeing | Confrontational ↔ Avoids confrontation | Is open disagreement in a meeting normal, or does it read as an attack? |
| Scheduling | Linear-time ↔ Flexible-time | Are schedules fixed and sequential, or fluid and relationship-dependent? |

## Practical calibration — the three counterparts this business actually has

**KRI/Iraqi clients (Middle East on most scales):** relationship-based trust (build rapport before business, patience across multiple meetings), indirect negative feedback (never contradict bluntly, especially in front of others), flexible scheduling (meetings run long, punctuality is a courtesy not a contract), hierarchical leading (decisions often route through one senior figure even in "flat" small businesses). **Adjustment:** don't rush to the pitch; let the relationship stage run its course. Face-saving (Carnegie's "let the other person save face") is not optional here — it's the operating norm.

**Western NGO/foundation staff (US/EU-leaning):** task-based trust (competence demonstrated fast matters more than rapport), low-to-mid context communication (say the thing directly, don't expect subtext to carry it), consensual-leaning deciding (budget approvals often need multiple sign-offs — ask who else is in the room early). **Adjustment:** lead with competence signals and clear deliverables; don't over-invest in small talk before getting to substance — it can read as unfocused.

**Remote Western hiring teams (interviews, comp calls):** low-context, task-trust, often egalitarian-leaning leadership style, linear scheduling (calendar invites are commitments). **Adjustment:** answer questions directly and concisely; STAR+R structure (interview-prep/story-bank.md) matches the low-context expectation — state the point, then the evidence, not the other way around.

## The bridge-figure asset

Being a native Kurdish speaker who also operates fluently in Western business register is not just a biography detail — it is a sellable capability. Name the SPECIFIC mechanism you resolve (e.g., "a Western program manager reads KRI flexible scheduling as unreliability; a KRI partner reads Western directness as disrespect — I sit in the middle and translate both ways") rather than a generic "culturally fluent" claim. Specificity here does the same work as `brain-audit.md`'s "logo test" for positioning — a generic claim any bilingual person could make isn't a differentiator; naming the actual friction point is.

## Face-saving as a hard rule, not a nicety

- Never contradict, correct, or criticize someone in front of others — especially in KRI settings, but the principle generalizes. Route corrections privately or indirectly (Carnegie P4.2).
- When you must deliver hard feedback (a rejected direction, a missed deadline), open with genuine praise or ownership of your own comparable mistake before the correction (Carnegie P4.1/4.3) — this is not softening the truth, it's making the truth hearable.
- A "no" that preserves the other person's dignity gets remembered as professionalism; a "no" that doesn't gets remembered as an insult, regardless of how correct you were.

## Anti-patterns

- Applying one register everywhere (treating an NGO officer like a KRI client, or vice versa) — the single most common failure for a bridge operator.
- Mistaking a culture's tendency for an individual's guarantee — scales describe averages, not people; read the actual person in front of you (Meyer's caution, always).
- Over-indexing on rapport with task-trust counterparts (reads as stalling) or under-indexing on rapport with relationship-trust counterparts (reads as rude, transactional).
